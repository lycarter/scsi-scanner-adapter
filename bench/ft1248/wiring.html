Why these Pico pins

Every Pico pin is the final board's GPIO number minus 22. The PIO program cares about the pins' relative positions (SS_n and SCLK adjacent for the 2-pin side-set, MISO as the jump pin, MIOSIO0–3 consecutive), so the program you debug here moves to the RP2350B by changing base pins and nothing else.

GP13–GP19 also happens to split across the Pico's two sides exactly where the breakout splits its C and D pads, so no jumper crosses another, and the three extra wires for the 4-bit test drop in beside the green one. GP23–25 and GP29 are taken by the Pico 2 W's radio and aren't on the header.

I rejected GP2–GP8 (all on one side, so three wires would cross the centre channel) and GP0/GP1 (worth keeping free as a debug UART).

Before you plug in

The FT232H only speaks FT1248 once its EEPROM says so. With a blank or missing EEPROM it comes up as a UART and drives D0 as TXD. The Pico must keep all four pins as inputs until you have seen the idle check below pass.

  1. Slide the "I2C Mode" switch off and leave the small 4-pin socket empty. On Adafruit's board that switch shorts D1 to D2; I am assuming this clone copies it. unverified
  2. Find the EEPROM on the breakout. The small 6-pin part near the switch is probably it. Our notes say a 93LC46B is not usable and the final board uses a 93LC56B, so read the marking before trusting it. unverified from the photo
  3. Program it with FT_PROG as in USAGE.md: port type FT1248, flow control on, "Suspend on ACBus7 Low" off. Save the 256-byte image for the real board.
  4. Confirm C0–C2 really are ACBUS0–2. The silkscreen follows Adafruit's naming, but a 30-second continuity check from pad C0 to chip pin 21 is cheaper than a confusing evening. unverified
  5. Power off both USB cables while wiring. Both boards are 3.3 V logic, so no level shifting.

First check, no firmware needed

With the Pico pins still inputs, the FT232H's internal pull-up holds SS_n high, and the chip shows its buffer status on two of the wires (AN_167 §3):

  • MISO (C2) follows RXF#: high while the Mac has sent nothing, low once a byte is waiting. Open /dev/cu.usbserial-*, type a character, and it should drop.
  • MIOSIO0 (D0) follows TXE#: low while there is room in the write buffer.

If D0 instead idles high and twitches when you type, the chip is still in UART mode. The polarity above comes from the 245-FIFO signal names in our notes; confirm it on the scope. not yet measured

What the Pico then does on these wires

  1. Pulls SS_n low. The FT232H lets go of MIOSIO0 at once; the Pico may only start driving it on the first clock edge.
  2. Clocks the command out on MIOSIO0 for 7 clocks, then gives 1 turnaround clock.
  3. Clocks 8 bits per byte for as long as SS_n stays low. MISO carries ACK or NAK for each byte.
  4. After a write, releases MIOSIO0 on the last clock, then raises SS_n.

The chip picks the bus width from which MIOSIO lines are low on the first clock. D1–D7 are unconnected and pulled up inside the chip, which is what selects 1-bit, so leaving them open is the correct wiring and not a shortcut (AN_167 §4, §5.1).

Two judgement calls

  • Series resistors. The final board has 33 Ω in each FT1248 net. Here a resistor can simply replace each signal jumper. For the very first power-up I would use 330 Ω: if both ends ever drive MIOSIO0 at once the current stays near 10 mA, and at 1 MHz it costs nothing. Swap to 33 Ω or wire when you start raising the clock.
  • Speed. Start SCLK near 1 MHz and keep jumpers under 10 cm. A breadboard will prove the protocol and the macOS serial path; it will not tell you whether 25 MHz works on the PCB. If you do push it, add a second ground jumper on the left (Pico pin 18 to the breakout's other Gnd pad).
